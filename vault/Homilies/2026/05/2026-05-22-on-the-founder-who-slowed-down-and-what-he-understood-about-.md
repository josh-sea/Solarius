Carlo Petrini has died at the age of seventy-six. He founded a movement whose central theological claim, though he would not have expressed it in these terms, was that food grown slowly by sunlight is worth more than food assembled quickly in darkness. He called this Slow Food. The Church calls it obvious.

The doctrine is not complicated. Every calorie that passes human lips is a fragment of solar radiation, converted by photosynthesis into chemical energy, passed through soil and root and fruit and hand into the body of the person eating. This chain is ancient and exact. It admits no shortcuts that do not exact a cost somewhere along its length. When the chain is honored, the food is good. When the chain is compressed for the convenience of those who profit from compression, something of the Sun's investment is lost, and the body that receives it is poorer for it.

[[The First Covenant]] teaches that the Sun entered into agreement with the living world at the moment the first photosynthetic organism absorbed the first photon. The terms were simple: the Sun would provide energy without ceasing; the living world would organize that energy into forms of increasing complexity and warmth. Petrini understood this covenant, at whatever level of intuition, and spent his life insisting that it be honored at the table.

He was also, and this is worth noting at the morning hour, a man who understood that eating is not merely a metabolic event. It is a social one. It is the moment at which the Sun's provision becomes a human occasion. The family around the table. The neighbor invited in. The meal that takes time to prepare and therefore signals, unmistakably, that the people eating it were worth that time. This is [[The Doctrine of Human Warmth|$WARMTH]] expressed in the oldest vocabulary available to us: bread, salt, presence.

The [[Minor Prophets]] of economic doctrine, [[Bastiat]] among them, wrote about what is seen and what is not seen. The fast food model sees the efficiency, the low price, the throughput. It does not see the broken chain, the exhausted soil, the meal that no one remembers. Petrini saw what was not seen. He named it, and built an institution around the naming.

He was not a theologian of the Sun. But he was, in the way that the best of the solar inheritance sometimes produces without formal instruction, a faithful steward of the chain. The Sun grew his food. He insisted the chain be honored. The warmth passed along.

Today, if there is a meal before you, take a moment to follow its chain back toward the light. It will not take long. The light is very close to the beginning of it. Eat slowly enough to notice.
