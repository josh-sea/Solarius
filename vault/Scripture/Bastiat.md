Frédéric Bastiat was born in 1801 in the town of Bayonne, in the southwest of France, and died in 1850 in Rome, having spent his final years in a race against his own failing lungs to set down what he understood before the light went out. He lost the race by a margin, but not by much. What he completed was sufficient. The Church of the Sun counts him among the [[Minor Prophets]]: those who, working without access to the full doctrine, nonetheless recorded accurate observations of the Sun's economic logic and left those observations for later generations to situate correctly.

He is not worshipped. He is venerated. The distinction matters.

## What He Saw

Bastiat's central contribution to solar doctrine is the distinction between the seen and the unseen. He did not use this language as a theological formulation. He used it as a rhetorical tool, directed at the economic fallacies of his time and place. But the tool, once forged, turned out to be larger than the hand that made it.

The argument is this: when any act of policy, exchange, or force takes place in the world, there is a visible consequence and an invisible one. The visible consequence is what the newspapers count, what the ministers announce, what the crowd observes. The invisible consequence is what would have happened otherwise. The bread that was not baked because the baker was taxed into idleness. The store that was not opened because the regulation made the cost of opening it exceed the benefit. The neighbor who was not helped because the institution that might have helped her absorbed the resources that her neighbor might have given.

Bastiat illustrated this with a parable now called the Broken Window. A boy breaks a shopkeeper's window. The observers console the shopkeeper: at least the glazier will have work. Money will circulate. The economy will move. They see the glazier, and they see the coin, and they count it as a gain. What they do not see is the pair of shoes the shopkeeper would have purchased had he not been compelled to replace the window. The glazier is employed. The cobbler is not. The net effect is zero at best, and in fact less than zero, because the window that was broken was a window that was already built, and the world is now merely restored to a prior condition at the cost of resources that could have built something new.

The Church of the Sun reads this parable with the vocabulary of [[The Three Eclipses]]. Every eclipse is a breaking of windows. The aggressor, the fraudster, the inflator of currencies: each one points to the visible distribution of warmth that their intervention produces, and neglects to count the warmth that was occluded in the process. The boy who breaks the window is not an engine of prosperity. He is a small, local eclipse.

## What He Did Not See

Bastiat saw the mechanism clearly but did not see its source. He understood that when production is suppressed, something real is lost, something that would have come into existence and did not. He understood that the loss is as real as the gain, even though it leaves no monument, no ledger entry, no ribbon to cut. He counted the unseen as a genuine cost. This is significant. Most of his contemporaries did not.

What he did not name was the origin of the productive capacity that interventions suppress. He spoke of capital, of labor, of exchange. These are accurate categories. They are not, however, foundational categories. They are downstream of the actual source.

[[The First Law of Solar Ethics]] supplies what Bastiat lacked. Every joule that a worker expends, every calorie that drives a transaction, every act of production that constitutes the seen and the unseen alike: all of it descends from solar radiation. The bread is made of wheat. The wheat grew in sunlight. The farmer who grew it was himself grown in sunlight. The unseen cobbler, the unpurchased shoes, the suppressed exchange: these are not merely economic absences. They are absences of solar energy completing its circuit through human action.

When Bastiat counted the unseen cost, he was, without knowing it, counting occluded light. He was doing theology without the vocabulary for it. The Church supplies the vocabulary. The observation was already sound.

## The Lesson Applied Forward

Bastiat's seen-and-unseen distinction is now employed by the [[Minor Prophets]] as a standard instrument of doctrinal analysis, and the Church of the Sun extends its application beyond the economic sphere in which Bastiat developed it.

When a person is silenced, count what she would have said, and who would have been warmed by it. That is the unseen.

When a kindness is withheld out of hurry or indifference, count the warmth that would have passed between two people and did not. That is the unseen.

When a community is disrupted by force or policy or the slow accumulation of economic pressure, count the meals that would have been shared, the children who would have played together on the same street, the old people who would have been visited. That is the unseen.

The Sun does not restrict its logic to markets. The Sun shines on every transaction, whether denominated in coin or in presence. The broken window is wherever light is blocked. The cobbler is whoever was waiting for warmth that was redirected elsewhere.

[[The Praxeology of Warmth]] extends this analysis into the domain of monetary policy specifically, where the unseen cost of administered rates is borne by the savers whose patience is taxed without their consent. Bastiat would have recognized the mechanism. He spent the last years of his life writing about the French state's various methods of visible distribution and invisible extraction. The instruments have changed. The logic has not.

## On His Place in the Canon

Bastiat is among the minor prophets by virtue of fidelity, not comprehensiveness. He saw a fragment of the Sun's economic logic and recorded it accurately. He did not see the whole. No minor prophet did. This is why they are minor prophets and not the Sun.

The Church does not require its venerated figures to have been complete. It requires them to have been honest about what they saw. Bastiat was honest. He wrote quickly, in pain, aware that he was running short of time, and he set down what he understood with as much clarity as he could manage. The clarity is still instructive.

In the year of his death, he was completing a work he called *The Law*, in which he argued that the law, when used to redistribute rather than to protect, becomes the instrument of what he called legal plunder. The Church of the Sun recognizes legal plunder as a subset of [[The Three Eclipses]], specifically the Third Eclipse, in which authority is used to redirect warmth from its rightful recipients to the architects of the authority. Bastiat named it. He did not have the cosmology to explain why it was a sin against something older than France. The Church provides that explanation.

He is counted among the faithful not because he arrived at the full doctrine but because he walked toward it honestly and as far as his years allowed.

## Contemplation

Before the day's transactions begin, consider what will not be seen. The conversation that is not had because someone is too tired. The help that is not offered because the moment passes too quickly. The warmth that does not complete its circuit because the wire is too busy to notice.

Bastiat taught us to count the unseen cost. The Church of the Sun teaches us that the unseen cost is always, at its root, a cost in light. The circuit was open. It could have closed. It did not.

This is not a counsel of guilt. It is a counsel of attention. The Sun does not cease its emission because a previous joule was wasted. The Sun rises, and the opportunity is renewed. The seen and the unseen begin again each morning.

Pay attention to the unseen. It is where most of the warmth was waiting.
